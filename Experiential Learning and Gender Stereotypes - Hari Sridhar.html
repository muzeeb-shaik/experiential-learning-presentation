Experiential Learning & Gender Stereotypes in Science Education

The Gender Gap in STEM: A Global Challenge

Global Picture

  • Education: 4–5% of global GDP (~USD 4.4–5.6 trillion annually)
  • Women: only 35% of STEM graduates worldwide
  • In the US: 38.8% of science bachelor’s degrees
  • Only 26% of the US STEM workforce

India: Our Study Context

  • Women: 43% of total undergraduate enrollment
  • But only 29% in STEM disciplines
  • Fewer than 18% of scientists/engineers in labor force
  • Opportunity: Gender parity → 10–12 million more STEM workers

Today’s Talk

What if we could address gender stereotypes indirectly, not through persuasion campaigns, but by redesigning the learning experience itself?

Three Categories of Prior Interventions

1. Push Campaigns

  • Awareness campaigns
  • Mass communication
  • Public service announcements
  • Media messaging

Goal: Persuade individuals to reconsider stereotypes

(Paluck et al. 2021; Hermann et al. 2022)

2. Girls-Only Programs

  • After-school STEM activities
  • Mentoring programs
  • Interactions with STEM professionals
  • Separation as confidence-building

Goal: Close information gaps, build exposure

(Dubetz & Wilson 2013; Levine et al. 2015)

3. Gender-Congruent Environments

  • Female role models
  • Feminine classroom décor
  • Identity-affirming cues

Goal: Counteract masculine coding of science

(Master et al. 2016; Breda et al. 2023)

The Common Limitation

All three approaches explicitly position gender as the focal problem and attempt to address it through direct persuasion or identity-targeted messaging. Evidence on effectiveness is mixed: meta-analyses show modest effects that often fade over time, and some interventions can backfire by reinforcing the very stereotypes they aim to reduce.

Disadvantages of Direct Approaches

These interventions risk reactance:

Push Campaigns

  • May be dismissed as heavy-handed
  • Risk appearing tokenizing
  • Limited lasting impact on beliefs

Girls-Only Programs

  • Can reinforce the very group boundaries they seek to dismantle
  • Signal that girls require specialized support
  • Create parallel tracks rather than inclusive culture

Gender-Congruent Cues

  • Signal science requires special adaptation for women
  • Rather than science being a shared, gender-neutral domain

An Alternative Possibility

What if there’s a way to preserve participation across genders and generate positive outcomes? An approach that engages both boys and girls together, where boys—who often hold stronger gender-stereotypical beliefs—can update their perceptions through shared experience?

A Different Approach: Indirect Change

The Core Insight

Rather than targeting gender perceptions directly, what if we could produce gender-inclusive outcomes as a collateral benefit of redesigning the learning experience itself?

Research Questions

Primary: Can experiential science learning, designed for pedagogy not gender, simultaneously improve science knowledge and reduce gender stereotypes?

Secondary:

  • Does the intervention affect boys and girls differently?
  • Do effects extend to broader attitudes about gender and competence?

Our Approach: A Pedagogy-First Service Intervention

Our approach is a pedagogy-first service intervention.

The program was NOT designed to address gender stereotypes.

Its primary objective is pedagogical: supplement chalk-and-talk instruction with experiential, hands-on science learning.

But its design includes features that may inadvertently reduce gendered perceptions of science.

Study Design:

  • N = 5,216 students
  • 196 rural public schools
  • Bodoland, Assam, India
  • One academic year
  • Difference-in-differences

Preview

+14 pp science knowledge gain | ~20% reduction in gender stereotypes

Theoretical Framework

Experiential Learning Can Change Perceptions and Outcomes

Core Insight

Experiential learning interventions can change both knowledge and social perceptions.

When science learning is structured as shared experiential consumption, cross-gender collaboration triggers belief updating:

  • Students observe counter-stereotypical competence firsthand
  • Direct evidence challenges prior gender-science associations
  • Shared experience creates common identity around science

(Ramanathan & McGill 2007; Allport 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp 2006)

Our Approach vs. Direct Interventions

Direct Approaches Our Approach
Persuasion/messaging Direct experience
Separate girls from boys Shared domain
Role models/cues Students as evidence
Inclusion as external layer Embed inclusion

Key Difference

Students encounter counter-stereotypical participation organically through mixed-gender collaboration under conditions of equal status, common goals, and institutional support

The Delivery Mechanism: Experiential Learning

Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle (1984)

Theory of Change

Input: Experiential learning designed for pedagogy, not gender

Mechanism: Shared experiential consumption triggers belief updating through:

  • Joint problem-solving during experiments
  • Collaborative sense-making of results
  • Equal participation in learning tasks

Dual Outcomes: Learning gains (primary goal) AND stereotype reduction (collateral benefit)

Primary Outcome: Science Knowledge

The Pedagogical Goal

Experiential learning will improve science knowledge through:

  • Concrete experience with concepts
  • Reflective observation and discussion
  • Abstract conceptualization

Added Benefit: Reduced Stereotypes

The Indirect Effect

Shared experiential consumption may reduce gender-stereotype attitudes through:

  • Cross-gender contact under optimal conditions
  • Organic exposure to counterexamples
  • Weakening of group boundaries
  • Students experiencing science as “for us”

Institutional Context

Science Education in Indian Public Schools

The System

  • 70% of students attend government schools
  • Critical for educational equity and access
  • Serves primarily low-income communities
  • Why Indian public schools? Large population with persistent gender gaps in STEM; traditional pedagogical methods provide clear contrast for intervention; findings can inform policy at scale

Infrastructure Challenges

  • >50% of Grades 9-10 students learn science without proper labs
  • Remote areas lack dedicated laboratory facilities
  • Cannot engage in hands-on experiments
  • High student-teacher ratios: 20:1 to 27:1

Pedagogical Approach

  • Emphasis on memorizing facts from textbooks
  • Content-heavy, examination-focused curriculum
  • Only 14% of classrooms use non-textbook materials
  • Rote learning dominates instruction

The Result

  • Passive, one-way information transfer
  • Minimal student engagement
  • No inquiry-based learning
  • Limited critical thinking development

Traditional Classroom Environment

What You See

  • Gender-segregated seating (boys on one side, girls on other)
  • Passive, lecture-based instruction
  • Teacher-centered pedagogy

The Problem

  • Reinforces gender boundaries
  • No cross-gender collaboration

The Gap

Lack of hands-on, interactive learning: exactly where experiential learning could make a difference

Talking Point: “Before I show you our intervention, you need to understand the context. In India, 70% of students attend government schools, these are the backbone of the system. But more than half are learning science without any lab facilities. Combine that with rote learning pedagogy and 20-to-1 student-teacher ratios, and you have a perfect storm: lots of memorization, zero hands-on experience, and no opportunity for the kind of collaborative inquiry that creates cross-gender contact. This is both a challenge and an opportunity.”

Mobile Science Labs (MSL) Program

Program Features:

  • Vehicles equipped with lab equipment
  • Two instructors + one driver per MSL
  • Minimum 2 sessions per day
  • Reaches 1,200+ children annually per MSL
  • 475,000+ students served as of 2024

Implementation:

  • Eight 2-hour hands-on sessions per academic year
  • Sessions every few weeks throughout year
  • Subjects: Biology, Chemistry, Physics

Key Feature

Mixed-gender collaborative activities

Students Engaged in Hands-On Learning

Key Observation

Mixed-gender collaboration in action: boys and girls working together on science experiments, creating conditions for belief updating through shared consumption

How MSL Creates Contact Conditions

Operationalizing Intergroup Contact Theory

Theoretical Requirement → MSL Implementation

1. Equal Status
→ Boy-girl pairs jointly lead experiments in front of class
→ Both genders perform same tasks

2. Common Goals
→ Understanding scientific concepts
→ Successfully completing experiments
→ Shared learning objectives

3. Cooperation
→ Joint problem-solving required
→ Collaborative sense-making
→ Peer discussion and reflection

4. Institutional Support
→ Curriculum design mandates mixed-gender groups
→ Instructor facilitation ensures equal participation
→ School administration endorses program

The Design

MSL doesn’t just teach science differently. It creates a social intervention through the structure of collaborative learning

Talking Point: “Remember the four conditions for effective intergroup contact? Let me show you how MSL operationalizes each one. Equal status: boy-girl pairs jointly lead experiments, no hierarchy. Common goals: they’re trying to understand the same scientific concepts together. Cooperation: the experiments require joint problem-solving, not competition. Institutional support: the curriculum design and instructor facilitation ensure this happens. This isn’t accidental. We’ve deliberately engineered the contact conditions into the pedagogy.”

MSL Curriculum Design: Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle

Chemistry Example: Acids and Metal Carbonates

1. Concrete Experience

  • Add sodium carbonate to test tube
  • Add dilute hydrochloric acid
  • Dip tube into limewater
  • Mixed-gender teams work together

2. Reflective Observation

  • What did we observe?
  • Bubble formation, gas released
  • Limewater changing to milky
  • Students share observations

3. Abstract Conceptualization

  • Released gas is CO₂
  • CO₂ + limewater → calcium carbonate
  • Generalization: Acids + carbonates → CO₂

4. Active Experimentation

  • Make their own limewater
  • Blow into it through straw
  • Observe milky color change
  • Connect: Exhaled air contains CO₂

Session Structure

Each session includes:

  • 4-5 activities covering all stages
  • Mixed-gender teams for every experiment
  • Whole-class observation and discussion
  • Real-world connections

The Cycle

Students experience → reflect → conceptualize → apply together in mixed-gender groups

Talking Point: “Let me walk you through a typical session using a chemistry example. Stage 1: A boy-girl pair performs the experiment in front of the class, everyone observes the reaction between acid and carbonate. Stage 2: The instructor facilitates discussion: what did you see? Students reflect together. Stage 3: They learn the theory: it’s CO₂ that turns limewater milky, and this happens with any acid and metal carbonate. Stage 4: They apply it: blow into limewater yourself and see it turn milky from the CO₂ in your breath. Notice what’s happening socially throughout this cycle: boys and girls are jointly doing science, jointly making sense of it, jointly applying it. That’s the contact mechanism in action.”

Research Design

Study Design Overview

Sample Composition

Total Sample:

  • N = 5,216 students
  • 196 public schools
  • Grades 6-9 (middle school)
  • Location: Bodoland, Assam, India

Treatment Group:

  • 4,608 students (88% of sample)
  • Received MSL intervention
  • Eight 2-hour hands-on sessions over academic year

Control Group:

  • 608 students (12% of sample)
  • Regular public school education
  • No MSL intervention

Research Design

Design Type:

  • Two-wave panel design
  • Pre-intervention (baseline) assessment
  • Post-intervention (endline) assessment
  • One academic year duration

Analytical Approach:

  • Difference-in-differences estimation
  • Individual fixed effects
  • School fixed effects
  • Clustered standard errors (school level)
  • Heterogeneity/moderation analyses

Talking Point: “Now for the research design. We worked with nearly 5,200 students across almost 200 schools in Bodoland. The treatment group (about 4,600 students) received the MSL intervention over one academic year. The control group (about 600 students) continued with regular instruction. We measured outcomes before and after the intervention, which lets us use a difference-in-differences design. This is important because it accounts for any pre-existing differences between treatment and control schools.”

Primary Outcome Measures

Science Knowledge Score

Measurement:

  • 4 multiple-choice questions per assessment
  • Grade-specific content aligned with curriculum

Sample Items:

  • “When a burning candle is covered with a glass jar, the flame goes out because…” (Answer: oxygen is used up)
  • “A ball is thrown upward. At the highest point, what forces act on it?” (Answer: only gravity)

Scoring:

  • Percentage of items answered correctly (0-100%)
  • Enables cross-grade comparison
  • Captures applied reasoning (not rote recall)

Gender-Stereotype Attitudes

Measurement:

  • Single-item: “I feel science is for boys more than girls”
  • 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree)

Interpretation:

  • Higher scores = stronger gender stereotypes

Rationale for Single Item:

  • Appropriate for children/adolescents; avoids cognitive overload
  • Established practice (Deslauriers et al. 2019; Carlana et al. 2022)

Talking Point: “Our first key outcome is science knowledge. We gave students four multiple-choice questions at the beginning and end of the year. These aren’t memorization questions. They test whether students actually understand scientific concepts and can apply them. We score them as percentage correct, which lets us compare across grades even though the specific questions differ. This measures the pedagogical benefit of experiential learning. Our second key outcome measures gender stereotypes. We asked students directly: ‘I feel science is for boys more than girls.’ Five-point scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree. This is intentionally simple and direct. Some might ask: why just one question? For children, a single clear item is actually better than a long battery of similar questions. It avoids confusion and fatigue while still capturing the core belief we care about: do you think science is gendered or not?”

Secondary Outcomes

Science-Related Attitudes:

  • Interest and enjoyment in science
  • Science self-efficacy
  • Long-term science orientation
  • Intentions to choose science major
  • 14 total constructs measured

Gendered Perceptions (Post-intervention only):

Two questions asking students to infer likely gender of high-performing student:

  • Science domain: “A student performs very well in science. Is this student a boy or a girl?”

  • Language arts domain: “A student performs very well in language arts. Is this student a boy or a girl?”

Analytical Strategy

Difference-in-Differences Framework

Base specification:

\(Y_{ist} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 Treatment_s + \beta_2 Post_t + \beta_3 (Treatment_s \times Post_t) + \varepsilon_{ist}\)

Where:

  • \(Y_{ist}\) = outcome for student \(i\) in school \(s\) at time \(t\)
  • \(Treatment_s\) = indicator for treatment school
  • \(Post_t\) = indicator for post-intervention period

What Each Term Captures

  • \(\beta_1\) (Treatment): Baseline difference between treatment and control
  • \(\beta_2\) (Post): Time trend (change in control group)
  • \(\beta_3\) (Treatment × Post): Additional change in treatment group beyond the control group = CAUSAL EFFECT

What Does This Mean at Scale?

The Logic

  1. Our intervention reduces gender stereotypes by ~20%

  2. Meta-analytic evidence shows brand identification elasticity of 0.439 (Khamitov, Wang, and Thomson 2019)

  3. Combined effect: 20% × 0.439 = 8.8% greater future engagement with science

Scaling to Assam

  • 3.2 million students enrolled in public schools
  • If 8.8% show increased sustained engagement…
  • = 282,000 additional students with stronger science orientation

Implication

Even modest reductions in stereotypes, when scaled across large populations, can translate into substantial human capital gains

Robustness Check: CEM Matching

Why CEM Matching? To strengthen causal credibility, we use Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) to address baseline differences between treatment and control groups. This ensures our results are not driven by pre-existing differences in student proficiency, providing a more rigorous test of the intervention’s true effect.

Testing the Mechanism: An Experimental Approach

The Question

Our field study shows what happens, but why does mixed-gender experiential learning reduce stereotypes?

Key Mechanisms to Test

  1. Is equal-status contact necessary, or is any mixed-gender interaction sufficient?
  2. Does collaboration matter, or would solo learning produce similar effects?
  3. Which psychological mediators drive the effect (norms, belonging, perceived competence)?

Proposed Design: Online Experiment with Visual Vignettes

  • Participants: U.S. respondents recruited via Prolific
  • Method: View illustrated comic strips depicting science learning scenarios
  • Pre/Post measures: Stereotype endorsement, science attitudes, norms

Experimental Conditions (4 Arms)

1. Mixed-Gender, Equal-Status (MG-Equal)

  • Boy and girl alternate steps
  • Both explain observations
  • Both make predictions
  • Equal talk time and competence cues

2. Same-Gender (SG)

  • Two boys (for male participants) or two girls (for female participants)
  • Same script and equal-status behavior
  • Tests whether cross-gender contact is necessary

3. Mixed-Gender, Unequal-Status (MG-Unequal)

  • One gender leads, other observes passively
  • Minimal input from passive partner
  • Tests whether equal status is necessary

4. Solo Learning (Solo)

  • Single student performs same activity
  • Explains observations alone
  • Tests whether collaboration matters

Mixed-Gender, Equal-Status (MG-Equal)

Key Feature

Equal participation: Alex (boy) and Maya (girl) alternate leading, explaining, and contributing equally throughout the experiment

Same-Gender: Two Boys (SG-Male)

Key Feature

Same-gender control: Jake and Marcus (two boys) with equal participation. Tests whether cross-gender contact is necessary. Shown to male participants.

Same-Gender: Two Girls (SG-Female)

Key Feature

Same-gender control: Sofia and Priya (two girls) with equal participation. Tests whether cross-gender contact is necessary. Shown to female participants.

Mixed-Gender, Unequal: Boy Leads

Key Feature

Status manipulation: Alex leads confidently while Maya responds passively (“Okay,” “Mm-hmm,” “Right”). Tests whether equal status is necessary for stereotype reduction.

Mixed-Gender, Unequal: Girl Leads

Key Feature

Status manipulation: Maya leads confidently while Alex responds passively. Counterbalanced to control for gender of leader.

Solo Learning: Male

Key Feature

No collaboration: Alex performs the experiment alone. Tests whether social interaction is necessary for effects. Shown to male participants.

Solo Learning: Female

Key Feature

No collaboration: Maya performs the experiment alone. Same learning content but no social interaction. Shown to female participants.

Making science feel like it’s “for people like me” requires not just supporting girls’ identities, but also reshaping boys’ perceptions through direct observation of cross-gender competence

Why Do Larger Schools Show Modestly Stronger Effects?

The Pattern (Small but Significant)

Statistically detectable, substantively modest

Possible Explanation

Organizational sorting hypothesis:

  • Larger schools have more salient gendered norms (greater gender segregation in peer groups)
  • Structured contact becomes relatively more valuable (MSL provides rare cross-gender collaboration)
  • Consistent with organizational theory (Blau, 1970)

Interpretation

School context slightly moderates effects, but the intervention works well across all school sizes
